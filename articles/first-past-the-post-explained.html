Voting

First Past the Post Explained

The UK elects its MPs with one of the simplest voting systems in the world. Simplicity, though, comes with consequences that shape everything from safe seats to tactical voting.

TATom Aldridge19 March 20265 min read

Ask most people how a British election works and they will describe first past the post without knowing its name. You get one ballot paper. You put a cross next to one candidate. The candidate with the most crosses wins. That is the whole system, and its simplicity is one of the main arguments its supporters make for it. Its critics argue that the same simplicity produces results that do not reflect how the country actually voted. Both are right about the facts; they disagree about what matters.

The mechanics

The UK is divided into 650 constituencies. Each one elects a single MP. To win, a candidate needs a plurality of the votes cast in that constituency, meaning more than any other single candidate, not a majority of all votes. In a two-horse race a winner will always have more than half the vote. In a race with four or five serious candidates, a winner can take the seat with a third of the vote or less.

There is no second round, no ranking of preferences and no pooling of votes between constituencies. A vote cast for a losing candidate has no further effect on the national result, and a vote cast for a winning candidate beyond the number needed to win has no further effect either. Political scientists call both of these “wasted votes”, a term that describes their arithmetic effect rather than passing judgment on the voter.

Where it is used

First past the post is used for elections to the House of Commons and for local council elections in England and Wales. It is not used for the Scottish Parliament, the Senedd or the Northern Ireland Assembly, all of which use proportional or preferential systems, nor for local elections in Scotland and Northern Ireland, which use the single transferable vote. Mayoral elections in England returned to first past the post from May 2024, having previously used the supplementary vote.

Why the seat totals differ from the vote totals

Because each constituency is a separate contest, a party whose support is spread evenly but thinly across the country can win many votes and very few seats. A party whose support is concentrated in particular places can win seats efficiently. The 2024 general election illustrated both effects clearly. Labour won 411 of the 650 seats, about 63 per cent, on around 34 per cent of the votes cast. Reform UK won about 14 per cent of the vote and five seats. The Liberal Democrats, with about 12 per cent of the vote, won 72 seats because their support was geographically concentrated in seats they were targeting.

None of this is a malfunction. It is how a system built around single-member constituencies is designed to behave. Supporters point out that the system usually produces a clear winner and a government with a working majority, keeps a direct link between each MP and a defined area, and is easy to understand and to count. Critics reply that majorities are routinely manufactured from a minority of the votes and that millions of voters in safe seats have little practical influence on the outcome.

Safe seats, marginals and tactical voting

A safe seat is one that a party wins by a wide margin at election after election. A marginal is one that could plausibly change hands. Parties concentrate their money, staff and leaders’ visits on marginals, which is why some parts of the country see a great deal of campaigning and others see almost none. Boundary changes, such as those introduced for the 2024 election, can turn a safe seat into a marginal overnight.

Tactical voting is the practice of voting for a candidate who is not your first choice in order to prevent a candidate you like less from winning. It happens because, under first past the post, supporting a candidate who cannot win in your constituency has no effect on the result. Websites and campaign groups now publish tactical voting recommendations for individual seats, though their accuracy depends heavily on the polling they rely on.

Key facts

  • One vote, one cross, one winner per constituency
  • A winner needs more votes than any rival, not a majority
  • Used for Westminster and for local elections in England and Wales
  • Rejected alternative: the alternative vote, defeated in a 2011 referendum

Has reform been tried?

In May 2011 the UK held a referendum on replacing first past the post with the alternative vote, under which voters rank candidates and the lowest-placed candidates are eliminated until someone has a majority. The proposal was rejected by about 68 per cent to 32 per cent on a turnout of around 42 per cent. Since then, proposals for proportional representation have been debated at party conferences and in Parliament but no further referendum has been held. Any change to the system for Westminster elections would require primary legislation.

Whatever your view, the practical advice for a voter is the same: under first past the post, the contest that counts is the one in your own constituency. National vote shares tell you the mood of the country; the result in your seat is decided by the people who live in it.

Vote and seat figures are from the official results of the 4 July 2024 general election, rounded to the nearest whole percentage point.
