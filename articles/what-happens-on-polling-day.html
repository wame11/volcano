Voting

What Happens on Polling Day

Polling stations open at seven in the morning and close at ten at night. Between those hours a carefully choreographed process unfolds in church halls, schools and community centres across the country.

PRPriya Raman2 April 20265 min read

For most voters polling day involves a ten-minute visit to a familiar building. Behind that short visit is a system designed to be secure, secret and the same everywhere, whether the polling station is a primary school in Cornwall or a portable cabin in a Highland lay-by. Here is what happens, in order, from the moment the doors open.

7am: the doors open

Polling stations open at 7am and close at 10pm. Each is run by a presiding officer, supported by poll clerks, all of whom are employed for the day by the local returning officer. Before opening, the presiding officer shows that the ballot box is empty and seals it. From that moment until it is opened at the count the box is never left unattended.

You do not need your poll card to vote, though it speeds things up. What you do need in Great Britain is an accepted form of photo identification. The list includes a passport, a photocard driving licence, an older or disabled person’s bus pass, a PASS-scheme proof-of-age card and several others, and out-of-date documents are accepted as long as the photograph still looks like you. Anyone without accepted ID can apply in advance for a free Voter Authority Certificate from their council. Northern Ireland has its own list of accepted documents and its own Electoral Identity Card.

Inside the station

A poll clerk checks your name against the register and marks you off, then hands you a ballot paper. The paper lists the candidates in alphabetical order by surname, with each one’s party name and, if they have one, party emblem. You take it to a booth, mark a single cross in the box beside the candidate you choose, fold the paper and put it in the ballot box. Pencils are provided but you may use a pen. If you make a mistake, you can ask the staff for a replacement paper before you post it.

Ballots are secret. Nobody may follow you into the booth, and the staff will not look at your paper. Photographs inside the polling station are strongly discouraged because they risk revealing how someone else has voted, which is an offence.

The rules are deliberately the same in every polling station in the country. Familiarity is part of the security.The Civic Ledger explainer desk

Voting without going in person

Postal votes are sent out in the weeks before polling day. The voter fills in the ballot paper at home, seals it in the envelopes provided together with a signed and dated statement, and returns it by post or by hand. Postal votes must arrive by 10pm on polling day to be counted. Since 2024 anyone handing in postal votes at a polling station or council office must complete a short form, and a person may hand in their own postal vote plus those of up to five other people.

A proxy vote allows someone you trust to vote on your behalf. Applications usually close six working days before polling day, but an emergency proxy can be arranged up to 5pm on polling day itself in defined circumstances such as a medical emergency or an unexpected work commitment.

Key facts

  • Polling stations open 7am to 10pm; anyone queuing at 10pm may vote
  • Photo ID is required to vote in person across the UK
  • Postal votes must arrive by 10pm on polling day
  • Exit polls are published only after the polls close

Tellers, rosettes and what campaigners can do

Outside many polling stations you will see volunteers with rosettes asking for the number on your poll card. These are tellers. They are not officials, you are under no obligation to speak to them, and they are not allowed inside the station or to campaign at the door. They use the numbers to work out which of their supporters have not yet voted so that they can knock on those doors later in the day.

10pm: the doors close

At 10pm the doors are shut, though anyone already in the queue is allowed to vote. The presiding officer seals the ballot box slot, completes the paperwork that accounts for every ballot paper issued, and the box is transported to the count. It is also the moment the broadcasters’ exit poll is published; it may not be released while voting is still under way.

The count

At the count centre, usually a sports hall or civic building, staff first verify each box by counting the papers inside and checking the total against the presiding officer’s account. Only then are the papers mixed and sorted by candidate. Counting agents appointed by the candidates watch every table. Doubtful papers, where the intention is unclear, are adjudicated by the returning officer in front of the agents. A paper with a tick instead of a cross is normally accepted if the voter’s intention is clear; a paper with two crosses is not.

When the count is complete the returning officer tells the candidates the provisional figures. A candidate may ask for a recount if the result is close, and the returning officer decides whether to grant it. Finally, the returning officer steps up to the microphone and reads out the result in full, ending with the words that make the winner an MP: “I therefore declare that the said candidate is duly elected.”

Voter ID rules and postal vote handling rules were introduced by the Elections Act 2022. Check the Electoral Commission’s website for the current list of accepted identity documents before you vote.
