Devolved Elections: Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

The three devolved legislatures are elected on different days, with different ballot papers and different counting rules from Westminster. Here is how each one works.

DMDaniel Marsh14 May 20265 min read

Since 1999 the UK has had three devolved legislatures alongside Westminster. Each makes law on the matters devolved to it, such as health, education and much of transport, and each is elected separately from the House of Commons. What surprises many voters is that none of them uses the first-past-the-post system familiar from general elections, and none uses quite the same system as the others.

Scotland: the Scottish Parliament

The Scottish Parliament at Holyrood has 129 Members of the Scottish Parliament, elected by the Additional Member System. Each voter has two ballot papers. On the first, they vote for a constituency candidate; 73 constituency MSPs are elected this way, by first past the post. On the second, they vote for a party in one of eight electoral regions; 56 regional MSPs are allocated from party lists, seven per region, using a formula that takes into account how many constituency seats each party has already won in that region. The effect is to make the overall result in each region much closer to the share of the party vote.

The Parliament serves a five-year term. The voting age for Scottish Parliament elections is 16, and the electorate includes foreign nationals with leave to remain in the UK. The party or parties that can command a majority nominate a First Minister, who is then formally appointed by the King.

Wales: the Senedd

Wales has changed its system recently. From 1999 to 2021 the Senedd (previously the National Assembly for Wales) had 60 members elected by the same Additional Member System used in Scotland, with 40 constituency members and 20 regional members. Legislation passed by the Senedd in 2024 expanded it to 96 members and replaced that system with closed-list proportional representation. Under the new arrangements Wales is divided into 16 constituencies, each electing six members. Voters cast a single vote for a party list, and seats in each constituency are shared out between parties in proportion to their votes using the D’Hondt method. The new system was used for the first time at the Senedd election in May 2026.

As in Scotland, the voting age is 16 and terms last five years. The Senedd nominates a First Minister who is appointed by the King.

Northern Ireland: the Assembly

The Northern Ireland Assembly at Stormont has 90 Members of the Legislative Assembly, five from each of 18 constituencies, which are the same constituencies used for Westminster elections. They are elected by the single transferable vote. Voters rank candidates in order of preference, writing 1, 2, 3 and so on; they can rank as many or as few as they wish. Candidates who reach a quota of votes are elected and their surplus votes are transferred to voters’ next preferences, while the lowest-placed candidates are eliminated and their votes transferred in the same way until all five seats are filled. Counts can take more than a day.

The Assembly’s executive is built on power-sharing rules set out in the 1998 Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement and later legislation. The First Minister and deputy First Minister are nominated by the largest parties of the two largest designations (unionist and nationalist) and hold office jointly; neither can act without the other. If the posts cannot be filled, the executive cannot function, which has led to lengthy periods without a functioning government. Voting in Northern Ireland has required photo identification since 2003, and the voting age for Assembly elections is 18.

Key facts

  • Scottish Parliament: 129 MSPs, Additional Member System, voting age 16
  • Senedd: 96 Members from May 2026, closed-list PR in 16 constituencies, voting age 16
  • Northern Ireland Assembly: 90 MLAs, single transferable vote, voting age 18
  • All three normally serve five-year terms

What the devolved bodies cannot do

Devolution transfers specific powers and leaves the rest at Westminster. Defence, foreign affairs, immigration, most of the tax system and the constitution itself are “reserved”, though the precise settlement differs between the three nations and Scotland in particular has substantial powers over income tax rates and some benefits. The UK Parliament remains legally able to legislate on any matter, but by convention, known as the Sewel convention, it does not normally do so in devolved areas without the consent of the devolved legislature.

Timing and overlaps

Devolved elections are scheduled to avoid clashing with general elections where possible, and the law provides mechanisms to move a devolved election date if one is called for the same day. Local elections in Scotland and Northern Ireland use the single transferable vote, as their assemblies do, while local elections in Wales use first past the post, an example of how the map of voting systems across the UK has grown piece by piece rather than by design.

Details of the Welsh reforms are from the Senedd Cymru (Members and Elections) Act 2024. For any forthcoming election, the Electoral Commission publishes guidance on the system and the ballot papers voters will receive.
