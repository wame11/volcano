Parliament

Constituencies, MPs and the House of Commons

There are 650 of them, each with its own boundary, its own returning officer and its own single MP. Understanding constituencies is the key to understanding how Parliament is built.

CFCallum Ferris16 April 20265 min read

The House of Commons has 650 members, and each one represents a specific, mapped area called a constituency. Whether you live in a city-centre seat of a few square miles or a rural seat larger than some countries, the arrangement is the same: one area, one MP. This article explains where the constituencies come from, what an MP actually does and how the chamber itself works.

Drawing the lines

Constituency boundaries are set by four independent Boundary Commissions, one each for England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. They are required by law to review the boundaries periodically so that constituencies stay roughly equal in size. The most recent review reported in 2023 and its boundaries were used for the first time at the 2024 general election.

The rules from that review required almost every constituency to have an electorate within five per cent of the UK average, which was about 73,400 registered electors. A handful of constituencies are protected from this rule because of their geography: the two seats on the Isle of Wight, Orkney and Shetland, Na h-Eileanan an Iar (the Western Isles) and Ynys Môn (Anglesey). The 2023 review allocated 543 seats to England, 57 to Scotland, 32 to Wales and 18 to Northern Ireland.

Because populations shift, boundary reviews often merge, split or rename seats. A sitting MP whose seat is redrawn may have to seek selection in a different constituency, which is one reason boundary changes are politically contentious even though the commissions themselves are independent.

What an MP does

An MP has two jobs that pull in different directions. The first is representing their constituency: everyone who lives in it, whether they voted for the MP or not, whether they voted at all or are too young to vote. Most MPs hold regular constituency surgeries where residents can raise problems, and their offices handle large volumes of casework on matters such as immigration, benefits, housing and disputes with public bodies.

The second job is at Westminster: scrutinising legislation, questioning ministers, serving on committees and voting. Government MPs are expected to support their party in most votes; opposition MPs are expected to oppose. Party whips organise this, and defying the whip is a notable event. MPs may also introduce their own legislation through private members’ bills, though few of these become law without government support.

MPs’ pay and expenses are set and administered by the Independent Parliamentary Standards Authority, not by MPs themselves. The basic salary was set at £93,904 from April 2025; ministers and some office-holders receive additional salaries.

The chamber

The Commons chamber is deliberately adversarial in layout: government benches on one side, opposition on the other, separated by two red lines that members are traditionally not supposed to cross during debate. The Speaker sits at one end, presiding over proceedings and keeping order. The Speaker is an MP elected by other MPs and, by convention, gives up party affiliation on taking the chair. The major parties do not usually stand candidates against the Speaker at general elections.

The chamber has fewer seats than there are MPs, so on big occasions such as Prime Minister’s Questions or a Budget statement members stand at the bar of the House or crowd the gangways. There is no allocated seating, only the convention that the front benches belong to ministers and their shadows.

Key facts

  • 650 constituencies: England 543, Scotland 57, Wales 32, Northern Ireland 18
  • Boundaries set by four independent Boundary Commissions
  • Most electorates within five per cent of the UK average
  • A by-election fills a vacancy between general elections

Vacancies, by-elections and recall

If an MP dies, resigns or is disqualified, a by-election is held in that constituency alone. Technically an MP cannot resign; instead they apply for one of two ancient offices, Crown Steward and Bailiff of the Chiltern Hundreds or of the Manor of Northstead, which disqualifies them from sitting. The Recall of MPs Act 2015 added another route: if an MP is sentenced to prison, suspended from the House for at least ten sitting days or convicted of providing false expenses claims, a recall petition is opened, and if at least ten per cent of registered electors sign it the seat is vacated and a by-election follows.

The other House

Parliament has a second chamber, the House of Lords, whose members are appointed rather than elected. It revises legislation and can delay most bills but cannot ultimately block them, and it has no power over financial measures. Governments are formed and fall in the Commons because that is the elected House, which is why the constituency system, for all its quirks, sits at the centre of British democracy.

Electorate quota and seat allocations are from the 2023 boundary review reports. MPs’ salary is as published by IPSA for 2025–26.
