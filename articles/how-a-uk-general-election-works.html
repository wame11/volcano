Elections

How a UK General Election Works, Step by Step

From the moment Parliament is dissolved to the first Prime Minister’s Questions of a new term, here is the full sequence of events that turns a nation’s votes into a government.

EWEleanor Whitcombe12 March 20265 min read

A general election is the one moment when every seat in the House of Commons is up for grabs at the same time. It is often described as a single national event, but it is really 650 separate contests, run by 650 local returning officers, that happen to take place on the same day. What follows is the sequence in the order it happens.

1. The clock starts: dissolution

Under the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, a Parliament lasts a maximum of five years from the day it first met. If it is not dissolved sooner, it dissolves automatically at that point. In practice most elections are called earlier: the Prime Minister asks the King to dissolve Parliament, and the monarch does so by proclamation. The 2022 Act restored this arrangement after the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 was repealed.

Once Parliament is dissolved there are no MPs. Ministers stay in post so that the country continues to be governed, but the people who were MPs the day before are simply candidates, if they are standing again, or private citizens if they are not. Polling day is fixed at 25 working days after dissolution, which is why an election campaign is about five to six weeks long.

2. The writs and the nominations

Writs are issued for each constituency, instructing the returning officer to hold an election. Candidates have a short window to submit nomination papers, which must be signed by ten electors in the constituency, and to pay a deposit of £500. The deposit is returned to any candidate who wins at least five per cent of the valid votes cast. Parties are registered with the Electoral Commission, which is the independent body that regulates party finance, sets guidance for the running of elections and reports on how they went.

Anyone who wants to vote must be on the electoral register. Registration closes 12 working days before polling day. The deadlines for new postal vote applications (11 working days) and most proxy vote applications (6 working days) fall shortly afterwards.

3. The campaign

The campaign period is governed by spending limits set in law, both for national parties and for individual candidates. Broadcasters must observe rules on due impartiality. Local campaigning is intensely practical: leaflets, canvassing on doorsteps, hustings in village halls and a great deal of data work aimed at identifying supporters and persuading them to turn out.

A general election is not one contest but 650. The national result is simply the sum of what happens in every constituency.The Civic Ledger explainer desk

4. Polling day

Polling stations open at 7am and close at 10pm. Anyone in the queue at 10pm is entitled to vote. Voters in Great Britain must show an accepted form of photo identification to vote in person, a requirement introduced by the Elections Act 2022; Northern Ireland has required photo ID since 2003. Each voter receives a single ballot paper listing the candidates in alphabetical order by surname, marks one cross, folds the paper and drops it into a sealed box. We cover the day itself in more detail in What Happens on Polling Day.

5. The count

When the polls close, the sealed boxes are taken to a count centre. Staff first verify that the number of papers in each box matches the number issued, then sort and count the papers by candidate. Most constituencies declare overnight, with the first results typically arriving before midnight and the vast majority by breakfast. Close results can be recounted at the request of a candidate, at the returning officer’s discretion.

The winning candidate in each constituency is the one with the most votes. There is no requirement to reach a majority of the votes cast, a system known as first past the post. The returning officer announces the result in a formal declaration, and the winner becomes the constituency’s MP from that moment.

Key facts

  • 650 constituencies, each electing one MP
  • Maximum five-year term under the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022
  • Polling day is 25 working days after dissolution
  • 326 seats are needed for an overall majority in the Commons
  • Polls open 7am to 10pm; photo ID required to vote in person

6. From results to government

With 650 seats, a party needs 326 to hold an overall majority. If one party crosses that line the picture is clear: if it was already in government, the Prime Minister carries on; if it was in opposition, the sitting Prime Minister resigns and the King invites the leader of the winning party to form a government. Where no party has a majority, a “hung parliament”, the sitting Prime Minister remains in office until it is clear who can command the confidence of the Commons, which can involve talks about a coalition or a looser agreement. The whole process is set out in How a Government Is Formed.

Anyone who has worked on a campaign will tell you that the mechanics matter as much as the message. Candidates quickly learn that to win a marginal seat you have to play the game of local campaigning: knowing which streets to knock, which issues resonate in which wards and when to stop persuading and start reminding people to vote.

7. The new Parliament meets

The new Parliament assembles a few days after polling day. MPs first elect a Speaker (or re-elect the previous one), then swear the oath of allegiance or make a solemn affirmation. The formal opening follows, at which the King reads a speech written by the government setting out its legislative plans. The debate on that speech, and the vote at the end of it, is the first real test of whether the new government commands a majority.

What may change

The voting age for UK general elections is currently 18. The UK government announced in 2025 that it intends to lower it to 16 across the UK, matching the age already used for Scottish Parliament and Senedd elections. Whether that applies to any particular election depends on the relevant legislation having been passed and brought into force, so check the Electoral Commission’s website for the position at the time you read this.
